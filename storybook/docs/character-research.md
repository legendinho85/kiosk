# Choosing our lead character: what the research says

*Made Happy, 24 September 2026. Working paper for the founder. Nothing here is final until we agree it together.*

Confidence marks: **H** = high (several credible sources agree), **M** = medium (one credible source, or sources disagree on the numbers), **L** = low (a retailer or blog claim, or a search snippet only). **(unverified)** = from general knowledge, not checked this session. See [Limits of this research](#7-limits-of-this-research) before relying on any single number.

---

## 1. The answer in brief

1. **Keep a friendly animal, but make it the child's best friend, not the hero.** The named child is the hero of each job day and the animal goes along with them. Children take lessons more readily from human characters, and preschoolers learn words better from the personalised parts of a book. This set-up also keeps us out of Bizzy Bear's format, where the bear does the job. (H)
2. **The teddy's appeal comes from its baby-like shape, not from being a bear.** Big head, big low-set eyes, round cheeks, round body, short limbs. We can give those features to another animal. (H)
3. **The popular species are the crowded ones.** Bears, dogs, rabbits, mice, cats and pigs lead the bestseller lists, and each is already owned by a giant: Bizzy Bear, Spot/Bluey/PAW Patrol, Miffy/Peter Rabbit, Maisy, Pete the Cat and Peppa. Four species already do jobs in toddler books: bear, mouse, monkey and dog. (H)
4. **The best open lanes with teddy-level comfort:**
   - a **cuddly soft toy that comes to life**. No jobs series uses one, and it matches the "most kids have a teddy" idea exactly.
   - a **baby elephant**, a nursery and comforter staple. It has no owner in the jobs niche, only Elmer's look to avoid.
   - a **lamb** or a **hippo**.

   The **bunny** is the most popular choice but also the most crowded. A **koala** looks the most like a teddy, but it reads as a bear.
5. **My recommendation:** **Mallow, a cuddly toy lamb who comes to life**, with **Nimble, a baby elephant**, as a close runner-up. Show parents quick sketches of both before we commit. All six options are in [`character-options.md`](character-options.md).
6. **Build the repetition from a sentence or question, not a sound word.** Every big seller does this: "Where's Spot?", "That's not my…", "I love you this much". So drop "tip-tap". Use a line the child can join in with, answered by their own name, for example "Who can help? *{name}* can help!". (M)
7. **The ten jobs.** The strongest themes are Digger, Firefighter, Footballer, Christmas helper, Vet, Doctor/nurse, Train driver, Farmer, Bin lorry and Astronaut, with Police officer and Postie close behind (section 5). We should agree the ten and what happens on each page **before** writing any more stories.
8. **Names are not cleared yet.** This session ran out of web searches before the name checks, so every name in the options paper is **unverified**. Before we commit, we need a web search, an Amazon UK check and a UKIPO/EUIPO trade mark search (classes 9, 16, 28 and 41).

## 3. What makes a toddler character work

| # | Rule | Evidence | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Draw it as a baby.** Make the head about 40–50% of body height, with big low-set eyes, round cheeks, a tiny nose and mouth, a round body and short limbs | Children aged 3–6 rate baby-faced human, dog and cat faces as cuter and look at them longer (Borgi et al. 2014). Teddy bears changed over time towards bigger heads and shorter snouts (Hinde & Barden 1985), driven by the adults who buy them (Morris et al. 1995). Mickey Mouse was redrawn in the same direction (Gould 1979) | H |
| 2 | **Keep it round and soft** | Babies look longer at curved shapes, and 3-year-olds prefer curved toys to angular ones | M |
| 3 | **Give it one signature colour or mark, and a clear silhouette** | Spot's yellow and brown, Peppa's pink, Elmer's patchwork, Miffy's dot eyes. A character changing costume in all ten books needs its identity in body colour and shape, not in clothes. Test it filled solid black, and at Amazon thumbnail size (about 100px) | M |
| 4 | **Keep the drawing flat and simple** | Miffy is "as simple as possible". Peppa is built from simple shapes. Hey Duggee uses flat shapes. Simple shapes also make a plush toy possible later | M |
| 5 | **Use a short name a toddler can say** | Spot, Maisy, Miffy, Bluey, Kipper, Peppa: one or two syllables. Our extra rule: **it must not sound like a child's name**, because the app says the child's name next to the character's | M |
| 6 | **Make it a toddler stand-in** | It is curious, has lots of firsts, gets small things wrong, and puts them right with help | M |
| 7 | **Repeat a sentence, not a sound** | "Where's Spot?", "That's not my…", "He was too…! I sent him back", "I love you this much", "Did Pete cry? Goodness, no!", "I want my mummy!". BookTrust says repetition and predictability help children join in | M |
| 8 | **Keep the same small cast in every book** | Peppa, Hey Duggee and Maisy all use a hero plus friends of different species. Three-year-olds remembered new words a week later only when the **same** three books were reread, not nine different ones (Horst et al. 2011) | M |
| 9 | **Make the child the hero and the animal the friend** | Preschoolers shared more after stories with human characters than with animals acting like humans (Larsen et al. 2018). Children learned target words better in the personalised parts of a book (Kucirkova et al. 2014) | H |
| 10 | **Choose the gender on purpose** | Male animal characters outnumber female ones about 2 to 1, and about 2% are called "it" (The Pudding, 2025). Bears, dogs, foxes, frogs and monkeys are usually male; rabbits, pigs, cats, birds and ducks usually female. Making everyone neutral can end up erasing female characters (Walsh, 2026). Our house rule already keeps pronouns out of the text, so balance the gender of the friends on purpose | H |

### 3.1 Why "most kids have a teddy" matters, and how to borrow it without a bear

- Secondary sources say **60–70%** of UK and US children have a comfort object, but measured rates are lower: **27–36%** in one twin study (M).
- Children treat that toy as irreplaceable. In a Bristol/Yale study, 3–6-year-olds refused apparently identical copies of it (H).
- In a 2012 Travelodge poll of 6,000 British adults, **51%** still had their childhood teddy and **35%** still slept with one. It's a PR poll, but it shows the buyers' nostalgia (L).
- After bears, the **bunny** is the top comfort toy. Jellycat's Bashful Bunny is described as its bestseller of all time, and Jellycat's revenue rose 66% to £333m in 2024. **Elephant and lamb** comforters are the other nursery staples (L; this is retail evidence, as no survey by species exists).
- **What this means for us:** either (a) the hero *is* a cuddly toy, or (b) the hero is a baby animal drawn with teddy proportions from the comforter family (elephant, lamb). Both options are in the options paper.

**Proposed launch 10, for discussion** (from the careers report): Digger, Firefighter, Footballer, Christmas helper, Vet, Doctor/nurse, Train driver, Bin lorry, Astronaut, and Police officer *or* Postie. That gives four work vehicles, two caring jobs, one sport, one adventure, one community helper and one seasonal title, which balances the interests stereotyped as "boy" and "girl". Possible swaps: Farmer for Train driver, Postie for Police, Bus driver for Train driver (to aim younger).

**Christmas timing (my judgement).** A Christmas title needs to be live on Amazon by about **31 October 2026** to catch the gift season, which is about five weeks from today. We don't have a hero yet, let alone 24 illustrated pages, so that is very tight. We should decide together whether the Christmas book is realistic for 2026 or better as the lead title for autumn 2027.

**Rules for every job book:** use gender-neutral job titles (firefighter, police officer, postie, bin crew), show mixed crews of diverse human workers, and keep the peril low (a stuck kite, a lost teddy, a sore paw, each fixed on the same page). End every book at bedtime.

## 6. What this means for our hero

1. **Friend, not hero.** The child ("you", named, never drawn) does the job. The animal is their best friend and helps.
2. **Teddy shape.** Head about half of body height, big low-set eyes, round cheeks, round body, short limbs, and soft plush-like texture.
3. **One body colour, one fixed mark** that survives ten costumes, and a silhouette you can read at 100px.
4. **Name rules.** One or two syllables, early sounds (b, p, m, n, d, w), not a common UK child's name, no UK slang meaning, nothing that echoes "Bizzy", "Pip" or "Kipper", and checked on UKIPO and Amazon.
5. **Repetition from a sentence frame answered with the child's name**, the same in all ten books and changing at the climax. No sound-word chants.
6. **A small recurring cast** of different species, with the gender balanced on purpose, and including one early-word animal (duck, puppy or goose).
7. **Distinct from Bizzy Bear everywhere:** not a bear, not yellow, no push-pull-slide look, no borrowed title words.

## 7. Limits of this research

- **Every page fetch was blocked by the network proxy** (publisher sites, Amazon, Wikipedia, journals, NielsenIQ), and this session's web-search budget (200) **ran out before the character names could be checked**. All figures come from search-result snippets, and several are publisher claims that conflict: Bizzy Bear, Pip and Posy, Maisy, Kipper, Dear Zoo.
- No UK survey was found that ranks children's comfort toys by species. The "bunny, elephant, lamb" point rests on retail evidence (L).
- Market "saturation" ratings for species on KDP are judgements, not counts. Nobody ran an Amazon.co.uk result count.
- Claims marked **(unverified)** come from general knowledge, for example Lambie in *Doc McStuffins* and The Koala Brothers. The claim that Nosy Crow has bought a superhero-koala series came from one research report with no source link, so treat it as **unverified**.
- **Before committing**, we need to: web-search each shortlisted name with its species; check Amazon UK for the name in children's books; search UKIPO and EUIPO in classes 9, 16, 28 and 41; and check the ONS baby-name lists for clashes.
